You can incorporate a UK company online, before lunch, without speaking to
anybody. Working out what the law then expects of your website takes rather
longer, which is why most new businesses skip it and hope.

## What does a UK business website legally have to include?

A UK limited company's website must disclose four things: its registered
company name, the part of the UK where it is registered, its company number,
and the address of its registered office. Separately, e-commerce law requires
any commercial website to make its name, a geographic address and a working
email address easily and permanently available, plus its VAT number where its
activity is subject to VAT. And if the site collects personal data at all,
which a contact form does, you need a privacy notice that meets Article 13 of
the UK GDPR. Worth knowing what is not on that list: nothing in UK law
requires a business website to publish terms and conditions. They are a good
idea, not a legal duty.

## The footer that can lose you a court case

The trading disclosures come from regulations 24 and 25 of the Company,
Limited Liability Partnership and Business (Names and Trading Disclosures)
Regulations 2015, made under section 82 of the Companies Act 2006. The
regulations say the information must appear on your websites. They do not say
where, so a site-wide footer or a clearly signposted legal-information page
does the job. They do ask, charmingly, that it be in characters that can be
read with the naked eye.

Miss it and the company and every officer in default commit an offence. That
is the boring risk. The sharp one is section 83 of the Companies Act 2006: if a
company in breach sues to enforce a contract it made while in breach, and the
other side shows the breach caused it a loss or blocked a counterclaim, the
court dismisses your claim unless it considers it just and equitable to
continue. You can lose the ability to chase your own unpaid invoice because of
a missing footer.

No incantation required. Four facts, in a footer, ten minutes.

## The requirement almost nobody has heard of: a real email address

Regulation 6 of the Electronic Commerce (EC Directive) Regulations 2002
requires a commercial website to make available, in a form that is "easily,
directly and permanently accessible", the provider's name, the geographic
address where it is established, and details "including his electronic mail
address" that allow a customer to make contact rapidly and communicate
directly and effectively.

The email address is named in the statute, so a contact form on its own does
not discharge the duty. The form is fine as the rapid-contact route, but a
published address has to sit alongside it. This applies to ordinary brochure
sites, not just online shops, which is why so many otherwise tidy
small-business sites are quietly non-compliant. Since 6 April 2025 the
Competition and Markets Authority can enforce these duties directly, under the
Digital Markets, Competition and Consumers Act 2024, so they have teeth they
spent two decades without.

## The data rules moved twice in 2026

If your site has an enquiry form you are collecting personal data, so Article
13 of the UK GDPR applies: your notice must give your identity, the purposes
and legal basis, who receives the data, how long you keep it and the
individual's rights, in plain language, where you collect the data rather than
buried three clicks away. Since 19 June 2026 it must also tell people they can
complain to you and not only to the Information Commissioner, and section 164A
of the Data Protection Act 2018 now requires you to facilitate those
complaints, acknowledge one within 30 days and respond without undue delay. If
your notice was written before the summer, it is missing a line it is legally
required to carry.

Cookies changed earlier in the year, so check the date on anything else you
read about them. On 5 February 2026 regulation 6 of the Privacy and Electronic
Communications Regulations 2003 was replaced and the exceptions moved into a
new Schedule A1, which is why you may have heard that analytics no longer
needs consent. It is narrower than that. The new statistical exception applies
only where the sole purpose is collecting statistics about how your site is
used in order to improve it, the information is not shared with anyone except
to help you make those improvements, you tell visitors clearly what you are
doing, and you give them a simple, free way to object which they have not
used. Analytics that feeds advertising, measures conversions for an ad
platform or tracks individual visitors still needs consent, so a default
install with ad features on is not covered. The ceiling for getting regulation
6 wrong is now £17.5 million or 4% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher,
where it used to be £500,000.

## From nothing to live and compliant, and why "later" is a bad plan

We took a brand-new UK travel agency from zero web presence to a live, legally
compliant marketing site: services sections, a tested working enquiry form,
WhatsApp and phone contact wiring, and the required Companies Act
trading-disclosure footer. We handled domain purchase, DNS, HTTPS hosting and
interim email routing too, so they had a working front door and a working
inbox from day one. That last part is not incidental: the email requirement
above is impossible to satisfy if nobody has decided who reads the inbox yet.
The write-up is in [the fast websites case
study](/work/fast-small-business-websites), and it is hand-built, so it costs
essentially nothing to run.

"We'll add the legal stuff later" fails for a dull reason. Later is when you
have traffic. Disclosures, the privacy notice and cookie behaviour are
cheapest while the site is being built, because they are decisions about
structure. Retrofitting a consent mechanism onto a live site, or working out
after the fact whose data you have been collecting since March, costs real
money and produces a worse result.

## What I'd actually do about it

Open your own site and check three things. Are the four trading disclosures in
the footer. Is there a real email address, not just a form. Does your privacy
notice mention the right to complain to you as well as to the ICO. If you are
a new company all three are probably missing, and all three are an afternoon's
work rather than a project.

Leave the cookie question until you know what your analytics is actually
configured to do, because that determines the answer and nothing else does.
And whether you owe the ICO the annual data protection fee is worth ten
minutes on their registration self-assessment rather than a guess in either
direction.
